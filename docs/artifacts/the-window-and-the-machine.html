The Window and the Machine

The app on your laptop does two completely different jobs. One is showing you things — and it owns none of them. The other is the single path by which a coworker can touch your real computer, and it is the most carefully fenced thing in the system.

Job one

A window shows; it does not hold

Everything you can see in the app lives on the server. The app is a view onto it. This sounds like a technicality until you close the lid.

Job two

The only path to your real computer

A coworker on a desk cannot reach your laptop. There is no route. The only way anything touches your real files is a daemon that lives inside the app you installed — and the building cannot call it directly. It has to ask through the window.

The fence

Three gates, and one of them the building cannot open

The path above is fenced three times. Two fences are on the server, where you can answer them from any device. One is on your machine, on purpose, and nothing remote can move it.

Presence

“Connected” means one specific thing

The building needs to know whether your machine is reachable — but only for one purpose. It is not a status light, and it must never become a reason a coworker stops working.

The invariants

What a window never does

  • Hold a desk token, a connector token, or a model credential.
  • Decide a permission. It renders the question and sends your answer.
  • Flip the switch on your machine because you tapped “always”.
  • Keep the only copy of anything.
  • Run the agent loop. That is the building’s, and it is why closing the app is safe.
  • Be required for a coworker to work.
  • Enforce a rule. A rule enforced in one window is enforced for one window.
  • Let the building dial into your machine. Requests travel down a line you opened.

One sentence

The app is a pane of glass with one door in it — and the door only opens outward, from your side, after you say so.
