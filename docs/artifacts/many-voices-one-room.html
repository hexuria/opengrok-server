OpenGrok · picture book · hard topic 10

Many Voices, One Room

Put several coworkers in one conversation and two hard questions appear at once: who speaks next, and when does it stop? Neither can be answered by a model. Both are policy, and policy belongs to the building.

The word again

“Room” means two different features

They look identical in a sidebar and share almost no machinery. Building them as one thing is the mistake this page exists to prevent.

The trick

A group is a coworker whose members are coworkers

The elegant move: do not invent a new kind of thing. A group is an ordinary coworker with an ordinary transcript. What makes it a group is a list of members and a different loop.

Who speaks

The round: pick, order, rotate

The naive design lets the model decide who talks next. It produces either silence or a shouting match, and it is unbounded. Instead the building runs rounds with a fixed, boring, entirely predictable policy.

Where this lives. On the server, in the harness, as a group loop. It was a client-side orchestrator in the product this rebuilds — which meant closing the window stopped the conversation, and a phone could not join one at all.

The prompt

Each member hears the room, not the whole history again

A member that gets the entire transcript every round costs the most and behaves the worst — it repeats itself, because everything it already said is in front of it as fresh context.

The hard one

Shared rooms: every policy question, asked twice

The moment a second person is in the room, everything that was obvious becomes a decision. These are the questions to answer before writing anything, because each one has a wrong answer that is invisible until it matters.

QuestionThe trapThe safe default
Whose grants apply to a coworker in someone else’s room?Letting the room’s host widen themThe coworker’s own owner’s grants. Always. A room never adds capability.
Whose budget pays?Charging the host for everyone’s coworkersEach coworker draws on its own owner’s pool.
Who can read the transcript?Treating the room as public to its members foreverMembers see it while they are members; leaving stops new content, not old.
Who raises a card, and to whom?Asking the host about someone else’s machineTo the coworker’s owner. Only they can consent for their coworker.
What happens when someone leaves?Deleting their messages and rewriting historyTheir coworkers stop; what was said stays said, marked as departed.
Can a room see a coworker’s desk?Sharing the viewer with the roomNo. A desk belongs to its owner’s tenancy, and the room is not in it.

The build order this implies. Ship groups first — they need no new identity model, no cross-tenancy anything, and they deliver most of the value. Ship shared rooms when you are ready to answer the table above in code, with a switch that keeps them off until then, failing closed on every endpoint.

The invariants

What a room never does

  • Widen what a member may do. Membership adds an audience, not a capability.
  • Let a model decide when to stop. Rounds and caps are the building’s.
  • Move a coworker’s budget to somebody else.
  • Ask the wrong person for consent.
  • Live in a client. Close every window and the room is still there.
  • Give one member the first word every round.
  • Re-send the whole transcript to every member, every round.
  • Expose one person’s desk to the room.

One sentence

A group is just a coworker with members, and the only genuinely new thing is deciding who talks next — which is exactly the kind of thing a building decides, not a model.
