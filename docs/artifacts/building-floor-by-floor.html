The Building, Floor by Floor

If we built the whole thing again from a blank page: four things that run, one of them a building with ten floors, and the rules that let any floor move out without the rest noticing.

The whole thing

Four things run. One of them is the product.

A window for people, a building where the coworkers live, a row of desks they type on, and — by your decision — a separate path to the person’s own machine. Models and other people’s services stay outside. Everything that matters is inside the building.

What changed from the last book. The gateway is no longer a neighbour across the street; it is the ground floor. And “control plane” stopped being one word — it is nine floors with names.

The rules of the building

Modular monolith: one building, but every floor could be its own house

Not microservices — one process, one deploy, one database. Not a big ball either — every floor is a self-contained module with a front desk, its own filing cabinets, and a notice board. The test of whether you did it right is on the right-hand side of the picture.

  1. One deployable, one database, one deploy.Start it with one command. There is no “which service is down”.
  2. A floor owns its tables.No floor reads another floor’s tables. Not for a report, not for a quick fix. It asks the interface or it listens for an event.
  3. Floors talk through interfaces, never through each other’s insides.An in-process call today; the same call over the network tomorrow, and the caller cannot tell.
  4. What happened is an event on the spine, written in the same transaction as its cause.A transcript is already an event log. A run finishing, a computer stopping, a card being answered — all events. Floors subscribe; they do not poll each other.
  5. Doors are thin.A door turns a protocol into a command and a result back into that protocol. It holds no rule. If a rule is in a door, it is enforced for one client and not the others.
  6. The kernel depends on nothing.Ids, errors, the shapes of events. Every floor depends on it; it depends on no floor and does no I/O. That is why the rules can be tested with no database.
  7. The extraction test is the definition.Any floor can move to its own process by swapping its interface for a remote one. If anything else has to change, the seam was fake.

Floor by floor

Each floor: what it owns, who asks it, what it announces

Every floor below is drawn the same way on purpose. Its rooms across the middle; who is allowed to knock, on the left; what it posts to the spine, on the right. If a floor’s picture needs a different shape, that floor is doing two jobs.

FLOOR 01

Doors — every protocol, translated to one language

People and programs arrive speaking five different dialects. A door speaks one of them on the outside and the building’s own commands on the inside. That is all it does.

FLOOR 02

Identity — who is asking, and on whose behalf

One identity for every door. The person who signs into the desktop app, the bot key a script uses, the org key that Claude Code carries — all the same floor, all one answer.

FLOOR 03

Conversation — the record, and the rooms it happens in

The transcript is the thing the window is a window onto. It is append-only, it is the event log, and an entry kind nobody recognises is kept and re-emitted rather than dropped.

FLOOR 04

Agents — the coworkers, and the loop that makes them think

A coworker is a record. The harness is the durable loop: assemble the prompt, ask Inference, get tool calls, hand them to Tools, take the results, go again — and if it has to wait for a human, it suspends. Suspension is a state, not a failure.

FLOOR 05

Tools — what a coworker may do, and where each thing gets done

Three rooms that today are one function. The registry knows what exists. The router decides where — desk, the person’s machine, or a connector. The executor asks Policy, overwrites identity, runs it, and turns a refusal into a result the model can read.

FLOOR 06

Policy — one question per room, answered every time

The window configures; the building decides. Policy is not checked once at sign-in; it is asked on every action. Four rooms, four different questions, and they combine by intersection — never union.

FLOOR 07

Computers — when a desk exists, and who may sit at it

Everything about a coworker’s computer that is not using it. Birth, readiness, sleep, death. Which kind. Whose. And the URL a human looks at to watch or take over.

FLOOR 08

Connectors & secrets — other people’s services, and the keys to them

A connector is a server someone else runs, reached through a standard protocol. We do not write them; we connect to them. The tokens that let us in, and the one-off secrets a person hands over for a single run, live here and nowhere else.

FLOOR 09

Autonomy — runs that nobody typed

The reason the building exists: a coworker keeps working when the lid is closed. Three ways a run starts without a person: the clock, an event, or a message to everyone.

FLOOR 10

Inference — the gateway, moved inside

Every model call in the building leaves through here. It picks the model, picks which of the org’s credentials to spend, translates to the provider’s dialect, streams the answer back, and writes down what it cost — real and counterfactual.

SPINE

The spine — what every floor stands on

Not a floor. The shared kernel and the plumbing. Every floor depends on it; it depends on nothing and knows no floor by name.

The other three that run

Windows, desks, and the person’s machine

These are separate processes because physics says so, not because of a design preference: a window runs where the person is, a desk runs in a container, and the person’s machine is the person’s machine.

WINDOW Client — holds nothing, shows everything

  • Transcript viewRenders the record and the live activity. Owns neither.
  • CardsShows a consent card; sends the answer to Policy. Does not decide, does not flip switches on the person’s behalf.
  • Secret entryMasked field → Secret relay. The value never lands in client storage.
  • Screen viewerEmbeds the URL that Computers.Screen hands it. One viewer, one source.
  • SettingsConfigures. The building decides.
  • Not here any moreThe in-box exec daemon whose source lives in the client repo today belongs with the desks. Consent logic belongs on Floor 06.

DESK Computer — a Linux box with a screen and a jail

  • exec service :1337Run, files, computer use. Bearer token minted by Floor 07.
  • host service :1340Am I up, who am I, what can I do. Polled by Lifecycle. Runs no model.
  • Virtual displayA framebuffer with no monitor. Everything the coworker “sees” is this.
  • Viewer :6080The display as a web page, for a human to watch or take over.
  • Browser + profile volumeLogins survive a restart. Cookies stay here. Closing the browser must not kill the desk.
  • Workspace jailEvery path resolves under one root, after symlinks. Survives hibernation on a volume.

MACHINE The person’s own computer — a separate path, by decision

  • Local-exec daemonInside the desktop app. Runs a command on the person’s real machine.
  • The switchOn or off, enforced by the daemon alone. The building cannot flip it. This is the local brake.
  • How it is reachedThe router pushes a request out through the window’s connection. Card first, every time, answered on any device.
  • What it is notNot computer use. Not a desk. Not available when the machine is off — and the desk carries on regardless.
